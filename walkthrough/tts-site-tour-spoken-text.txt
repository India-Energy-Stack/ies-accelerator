Welcome to the India Energy Stack documentation. This tour covers what the site holds, how it is arranged, and how to find things quickly.

Start with what the site is. It is the technical reference for building on the India Energy Stack, published as a GitBook. Not the full story of I E S, and not marketing — the schemas, the build guides, and the setup steps. The background and the strategy live in a separate report, linked from the home page.

The left-hand sidebar is the whole site. Everything sits in there, grouped into sections. Here they are, top to bottom.

At the very top, above the groups, two pages sit on their own. Getting Started is the home page. Propose a Schema is the form to use when the catalog is missing something.

First group: Schemas Overview. One page introduces the set, and then there is a page for each schema, written in plain language. This is the "why" layer. Read a page here to understand what a schema is for before looking at its fields.

Second group: Schemas. The biggest section, and the developer layer. Every schema family has a page listing its fields, its file locations, and every published version. Older versions stay on the site, nested under the current one, so a link never dies. Two pages in here are worth knowing by name. Term Taxonomy lists every term the schemas publish, in one long searchable table. External Schemas covers the schemas I E S uses but does not itself publish.

Third and fourth groups go together: Use Case Overviews and Use Case Implementation Guides. The same five use cases, described twice. The overview is for someone deciding whether to do it. The guide is for someone building it. Anyone writing code should go straight to the guide.

Fifth group: Pathways. This is the section people miss. Instead of organising by schema or by use case, it organises by who you are. There is a page for utilities, one for regulators, one for technology vendors, one for researchers, and one for the secretariat. Each sequences the whole site into an order that makes sense for that role. It is the right place to start when nothing else looks obvious.

Sixth group: Draft, clearly marked work in progress. These are ideas that were never finished. They are kept so the work is not lost, but nothing here is safe to build on.

Last: Concepts. The one-time setup every participant does, plus the deeper background pages behind it. It sits at the bottom of the sidebar, but in practice it is needed early, so it is not a section to save for last.

That is the map. Next, how to move around inside it.

The fastest tool on the site is the search box at the top. It searches every page and every table, including the field names inside the schemas. For a specific field, a specific term, or a specific standard, search beats clicking through the sidebar almost every time. On most keyboards it opens with Control K, or Command K on a Mac.

That matters especially on the Term Taxonomy page. The page has its own filter box, but the filter only works on one build of the site, not this one. Here, the search box at the top does the job, or the browser's own find-on-page.

Pages also repeat the same shapes. Learn a shape once, and any page of that kind becomes readable at a glance.

Every schema overview page has the same numbered sections, in the same order: scope and purpose, what it records, how items are identified, definitions, the standards it is based on, gaps where Indian standards do not yet exist, the records themselves, the field summary, how it fits with other schemas, and open questions. The field summary is always section eight. The honest list of what is still unresolved is always section eleven.

Every version page starts with a row of file links at the very top. That row is the most useful thing on the page. It holds the source file, the compiled schema to validate against, the semantic context, and a folder of worked example payloads. Copying an example is usually faster than reading the field table.

Every implementation guide ends with a checklist, without exception. The checklist is grouped by phase, and each item links to the page that explains it. Many guides also carry a "jump to the checklist" link right at the top, because that is what most people want first.

The pathway pages are organised as phases and numbered steps, each with advice, the prep work required, and links onward. They are long by design — the roadmap is for skimming, and only the step in hand needs opening.

A few smaller things are worth knowing.

Coloured boxes on a page are callouts. Blue is useful context. Yellow or orange is a warning, usually that something is work in progress or has a limitation. Those are worth reading rather than skipping.

Several pages carry diagrams — flowcharts of a process, or sequence diagrams showing two systems talking. The proposal lifecycle and the trading flows both have them.

Any table with a status column deserves attention. Some schemas are stable and were used in the pilot; others are still work in progress and are labelled as such.

The whole reference is also published as a single P D F, linked from the home page, for reading offline or searching in one file.

Where to start depends on what you already know. Three answers.

For an engineer with a task already defined: Schemas Overview, find the schema that matches, then follow it into the Schemas section for the fields.

For someone who knows the outcome but not the pieces: Use Case Overviews, pick the one that matches, then click through to its implementation guide.

And for anyone unsure where they fit at all: Pathways, and the page for their kind of organisation. It sequences the rest of the site for them.

One last habit worth forming. The home page has a section called "Where things stand". It describes the pilot as a completed, dated event rather than something running now. The documentation is careful about that distinction throughout, so a claim about something being live or available should read as history unless the page gives a date.

That is the site. The sidebar shows the shape, search finds a detail, and the repeating page patterns make anything readable quickly. Schemas Overview or Pathways is the place to begin, depending on what is already familiar.
