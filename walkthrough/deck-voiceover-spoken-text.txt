The India Energy Stack. A shared set of specifications that lets every system in the power sector exchange data, the way U P I lets any bank pay any other. An initiative of the Ministry of Power, with R E C as nodal agency and F S R Global as knowledge partner. Fourteen months on, the digital rails are built.

Five parts today. First, the reframing: what I E S is, and why it is simpler than it first appears. Second, documentation and integration — the GitBook, the schemas, how anyone contributes, and where I E S plugs into DigiLocker, A P I Setu and OpenForge. Third, the accelerator: six utilities, four bootcamps, live use cases, and the utilities in their own words. Fourth, governance and scale-up, including the proposal to M N R E. And fifth, the way forward.

Section one. I E S reframing. From building blocks to one adapter.

The problem is not a shortage of data. Utilities have added smart meters, rooftop solar, E V charging and demand-side tools, and every one of them produces something useful. The data stays trapped in systems that cannot read each other.

The cost of that is real. A single DISCOM often runs several metering systems and spends years making them talk. The same effort is then repeated across the country.

The diagram on the left shows why. Six systems connected by hand need fifteen custom connections, and each one is negotiated and built from scratch. On the right, the same six systems each carry an adapter at their edge. All speak one language, so any can reach any, with no centre and no bespoke pair.

I E S is a common set of specifications for sharing data across the power sector, and the closest analogy is U P I.

With U P I, money stays in the bank. U P I is only the shared rules that let any bank's app pay any other bank's customer, without a separate deal for each pair.

I E S works the same way. Energy data stays in the systems that already hold it. I E S is only the shared set of specifications that lets any system exchange data with any other, again without a separate deal for each pair.

The sector already has rules for what to report. I E S does not replace them. It makes the same data machine-readable, common and verifiable, so that two systems can act on it directly.

Everything I E S does reduces to three steps.

Register. Every participant, every meter, every solar unit gets a verified digital identity in a shared registry. Created once, recognised everywhere, with no repeat K Y C. Identities are revalidated on a set cycle and can be revoked, so the records stay current as meters and solar units change.

Discover. Before any data moves, the two parties find each other in the registry and check credentials automatically. Who are you, what may you ask for, and on whose consent.

Exchange. Data then moves on published schemas built on a uniform taxonomy. Every exchange is consented, signed and receipted, so what was shared, with whom, and why is a matter of record.

Three steps, every use case, every participant. Whether the data moves inline or in bulk is a single decision made at contract time.

In practice, all of that arrives as one adapter with two sides.

The outside is ready-made and identical for everyone — an I E S compliant interface built on Beckn ONIX. Nobody builds that part.

The inside is a one-time mapping between the utility's own formats and I E S, set up once by its vendor. In the pilots, that mapping was done in a single day.

Think of it as a U S B C port. The connector is standard whatever the device behind it. Every system keeps its own internals, and every system speaks one language on the outside — so any connects to any, with no bespoke pair.

Section two. Documentation and integration.

Seven distribution-side schemas are published so far.

Electricity Credential is a W three C credential issued per meter by the DISCOM, carrying customer identity, D E R details and the tariff profile. Meter Data is the core meter-data format, covering customer, interval and daily profile shapes. Meter Data Credential wraps Meter Data in a verifiable credential, attesting to who produced the readings, and where and how.

Meter Data Request is the query schema for asking for meter data, carrying capabilities and authorisation. Meter Data Request Credential wraps that request and proves the seeker is authorised to ask.

A R R Filing covers DISCOM regulatory filings. And Outage Notification, at draft version zero point one, describes one planned or unplanned outage — grounded in a real DISCOM outage-management system.

More are being added on the I E S GitHub and GitBook.

Those seven schemas are the digital rails under the demonstrated use cases.

The sequence runs left to right. First the shared schema layer. Then the use cases it enables: Smart Meter Data Exchange, the Consumer Energy Passport, the Consumer Meter Digest, and D E R Visibility. And finally the dependency map, showing which schema each use case actually draws on.

The point of the map is that the use cases share rails rather than each bringing their own. Meter data exchange draws on three schemas. The Energy Passport draws on one. Nothing is built twice.

All of this is published. Here is how to reach it.

And here is the documentation itself.

The catalogue is not closed. The contribution procedure is already live, on the R E C website under "Contribute to I E S".

Four steps. Open the Contribute to I E S link on the R E C India site, where the full procedure is published. Fill the form and submit the schema — each submission automatically opens an issue on the I E S Accelerator GitHub, in the open. The chief architect team then reviews and comments on that issue, iterating the draft and consolidating repetitions. And once agreed, it is merged as a canonical J S O N L D schema, versioned, on the I E S GitHub and GitBook, exactly as the existing seven were.

Every draft starts from the same master vocabulary: same identifiers, same terms, same credential pattern, same conventions. No schema redefines a field.

The proposal form is open to anyone.

And every submission lands in the public issue tracker.

The full I E S repository is public on OpenForge, the Government of India's collaborative-governance platform.

What is public is the whole of it: architecture primitives, implementation guides, and the J S O N L D schemas — drawing on I E C six one eight five zero, Beckn, W three C Verifiable Credentials and the Common Information Model. The repository opens live from the R E C website.

The integration with N E G D is working, not proposed.

The Consumer Energy Passport is issued as a W three C verifiable credential and pulled into DigiLocker on the consumer's own request. P V V N L's live integration was demonstrated in March twenty twenty-six.

A dedicated energy section in DigiLocker is planned with N E G D, which would put every citizen's energy documents one tap away, alongside Aadhaar and vehicle registration.

Behind that sits a working rhythm. Two Energy Credentials bootcamps ran with N E G D and nine DISCOMs in March. Pull-based integration and authentication were agreed there, and credential validity was extended to one year.

Why it matters: DigiLocker is how a digital public infrastructure reaches the citizen. The day the energy icon appears, I E S stops being sector infrastructure and becomes something a household can see.

A P I Setu is the government's A P I exchange, and Passport A P I s at three utilities are published there — Tata Power, A P E P D C L and D G V C L, with P V V N L to follow once its A P I is published. DigiLocker approvals are in process. Any authorised service can build against them.

Section three. The I E S Accelerator.

Fourteen months, in numbers.

Six utilities are live today, against an initial outlook of one. Four of them did the full adapter build, and G U V N L is now extending that adapter to its other DISCOMs in Gujarat. Data from a hundred and fifty smart meters is available for analytics. Sixty technology providers have been onboarded, from a hundred and ninety-eight applications. Eight use cases have been identified, five of which have completed validation.

And the number that matters most is zero. No bilateral integration agreement was needed for any of it.

Before I E S, every one of these exchanges required its own agreement and months of integration. Six utilities have now done it on one published specification — three public DISCOMs and three private utilities.

Four pilot DISCOMs carried the deep implementation: P V V N L in Meerut, A P E P D C L in Visakhapatnam, D G V C L in Surat, and Tata Power in Mumbai. Each installed the adapter in one to two days, then brought a first set of use cases live over about a month.

The table compares before and after, use case by use case. D E R Visibility: a solar unit registered with a verified identity, and grid-operator visibility confirmed — where before, no reliable sector-wide method existed. The Consumer Energy Passport: issued and stored in DigiLocker, shareable for a loan, a subsidy or an enrolment — where before, no portable verifiable consumer record existed. The Consumer Meter Digest: a verifiable consumption history, reusable across DISCOMs and States — where before it was manual and confined to one DISCOM. And Smart Meter Data Exchange: a DISCOM to A M I S P exchange completed with no bilateral integration agreement, where before each pair took weeks to months.

The build follows the same four moves every time.

Register. The utility claims its own namespace and keys in the shared registry. P V V N L signs as p v v n l dot org. Identity stays with the utility.

Issue. Credential issuance runs on the utility's own servers. Passports and digests carry the DISCOM's own signature, revocable by the DISCOM alone.

Connect. The adapter is wired to the utility's own billing, meter-data and C R M systems. One mapping, done once.

And then switch on use cases. Each is configured on the same rails, with no fresh integration per use case, per partner, or per State.

The whole arrangement is federated. A utility can run every component on its own network. Nothing is replaced, and no data leaves without a signed, recorded contract.

Tata Power, Mumbai. Bootcamp, the twenty-first to the twenty-third of May.

Three use cases complete. The Consumer Energy Passport, developed and published on A P I Setu, awaiting DigiLocker approval. Smart Meter Data Exchange, running live over I E S rails. And energy data exchange for D E R visibility, with fifty meters' data flowing from the A M I S P, FluentGrid, to the analytics provider, Trielectric.

The value added sits in that last line. Trielectric is building D E R visibility, market-ready prosumer signals, revenue and billing analytics, and solar-underperformance detection on those fifty meters — analysis the utility could not run before I E S.

A P E P D C L, Visakhapatnam. Bootcamp, the twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth of May.

The Consumer Energy Passport is live in the portal, with sixty-four consumers registered, forty-eight of them real. My Meter Data is live for fifteen smart-meter consumers, a subset of those registrants. Both A P I s are on A P I Setu, awaiting DigiLocker. And Smart Meter Data Exchange was tested successfully with the technology provider, Element Tech.

So sixty-four consumers can carry a signed Passport, and fifteen can pull their own meter data inside the portal. These are the first consumer-facing energy credentials at the utility, with a provider exchange proven end to end.

D G V C L, Surat. Bootcamp, the fifteenth to the seventeenth of June.

The Passport is live for twenty-five internal test users, with the A P I on A P I Setu, and is rolling out across G U V N L's three other DISCOMs, residential consumers first.

D E R Visibility was reframed here into a grid-impact pilot at a single substation, using net-meter data the utility already held — looking at excess injection, evening ramp-up, phase imbalance, hosting capacity and net-banking mismatch.

D G V C L also ran its own consumer campaign, producing creative and testimony videos in English and Gujarati, pointing consumers to passport dot g u v n l dot com and the one nine one two three helpline.

The reframed pilot turns net-meter data into grid intelligence, and opens a path to community battery storage, hourly green-energy traceability and surplus-energy settlement — while the Passport scales state-wide through G U V N L.

P V V N L, Meerut. Bootcamp, the twenty-second to the twenty-fourth of June.

Passport and Meter Digest set-up is complete, with A P I publication and DigiLocker integration to follow. Energy data exchange is complete, with a hundred meters' data shared from the A M I S P, Intellismart, to Trielectric.

And there is a third item, marked proposed rather than complete. P V V N L put forward a new use case of its own — outage publication, built on its own one nine one two C R M data. Guidelines are under review and implementation has not started.

A hundred meters feeding analytics, and a utility proposing a brand-new use case unprompted. That is the ecosystem extending the standard on its own.

That last point deserves its own slide.

P V V N L took the adapter it had installed for the challenge and began building an outage service from data it already held. It used its own one nine one two C R M outage data as the source. It published an outage-notification use case on I E S rails, on that same adapter. And none of it was in the pilot scope — the utility did it on its own initiative.

This is the clearest proof the standard works. Once a utility has the adapter, it can build what its own grid and its own consumers need. No new integration, no fresh specification, and no waiting for anyone else to define the use case.

One adapter, and a DISCOM builds its own service. That is the compounding return the task force set out to create.

The original order envisaged a single proof of concept. What happened was more interesting, and it split into two paths.

Four utilities went all in. Tata Power, A P E P D C L, D G V C L and P V V N L each did the full build: their own adapter, their own keys, multiple use cases on their own systems. That is the deep path, and it took thirty days per utility.

Three came for one use case. B R P L, T P D D L and P V V N L joined for a single use case, demonstrated live at the A I Impact Summit — one use case, with no full onboarding first.

One pilot in the original outlook became four full implementations plus three single use-case utilities: six unique utilities live today.

The lesson for the next cohort is that a utility can onboard whole, or start with the single use case it needs most. The standard carries both.

The proof so far, in numbers, from the thirty-day challenge.

Sixty-four consumers hold live Energy Passports at A P E P D C L, and fifteen of them also use My Meter Data. Twenty-five internal test users are live on the Passport at D G V C L, ahead of a G U V N L wide rollout. Three utilities have Passport A P I s published on A P I Setu. And one use case was proposed by a utility on its own initiative.

Three shifts sit behind those numbers. Verification is collapsing from days to seconds, because Passports and digests are checked against the DISCOM's own seal rather than by phone calls and scanned bills. Data is becoming a service, with providers at two utilities building analytics on meter data they could not have accessed before. And the standard is spreading on its own — D G V C L is extending its rollout to G U V N L's three other DISCOMs, which was never in the plan.

The recognition matters, but it is not the outcome. The outcome is six utilities running live use cases on a published specification.

Section four. Governance and scale-up.

Set against India's other digital public infrastructure, measured from programme set-up to first live transaction.

Aadhaar took twenty months, and became a statutory authority in twenty sixteen. O N D C took ten months to a five-city alpha. The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission was piloted in six Union Territories on announcement day and reached national rollout in about thirteen and a half months. Account Aggregator took sixty months. India Energy Stack took fourteen, and is live at six utilities across five States.

In those fourteen months I E S produced seven things: a strategy document, an architecture document, published specifications, a running live environment, six live utility engagements, a governance design, and an approved R F P. No other Indian digital public infrastructure delivered all seven inside its first mandate period, and none had to do it across thirty-plus licensed jurisdictions.

The Account Aggregator row is the cautionary one. The R B I master direction came in September twenty sixteen, the A P I specification three years later, and the ecosystem went live exactly sixty months after the direction. The mandate was never the problem. There was nobody to publish the specification, certify participants, or hold the budget.

I E S already has the specification. The next phase gives it the body.

The proposed governance fits in one frame.

The Ministry of Power owns I E S. Architecture, code and specifications are public property, and no firm acquires a claim on any of them. A Governance Committee approves specifications, budgets and stage gates. An I E S Cell runs the programme day to day — publishing and versioning specifications, granting I E S Ready status, and coordinating utility cohorts. Delivery partners build under contract: they deliver, they do not govern.

Certification sits at the same altitude. Any participant self-tests against the open environment as often as it likes. Then a single recorded, automated conformance run — the suite decides, not a person. And the resulting I E S Ready status is recorded in a public registry, per use case, per specification version.

Ecosystem consultation is structured but non-governing, and an independent annual audit is published.

Procurement is proposed in four tracks.

Track one, core I E S: one accountable firm, through the R F P, building and operating the shared rails — registries, adapter, schemas, tooling, dev kits and the test environment.

Track two, a technical programme management unit: a separate, smaller engagement that evaluates the core firm's deliverables and assists on specifications, certification and design. Never the same firm as track one, so the checker is never the builder.

Track three, forward deployment: R E C runs empanelment and certification, and stakeholders then engage the certified agencies through their own limited tenders.

Track four, shared use-case infrastructure, at a later stage and case by case. Nothing is bought before a use case earns it.

The commercial terms are disciplined: a two-year core term extendable annually to five at R E C's discretion, a fixed milestone-linked build price, a quarterly S L A linked operations fee, hosting at actual usage, and a published rate card.

Two asks have been put to M N R E, for P M S G two point zero. Both specifications are already in pilot with four DISCOMs, in Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh and Mumbai.

The first ask is to verify from signed records. The Consumer Energy Passport and the Consumer Meter Digest would enter the P M S G application flow. For M N R E, verification then happens at the start, from the DISCOM's own signed record, rather than by manual checks weeks later. For the consumer, one DigiLocker credential replaces repeated document uploads, and the system quoted is sized from twelve months of real consumption.

The second ask is one identity per installer. A L M M and empanelment lists become a queryable registry, giving each installer one verifiable identity that any DISCOM can check. At commissioning, the installer signs an installation credential, the DISCOM verifies it against the registry, and the DISCOM then issues the Passport with the system details. Subsidy release can be tied to a verified installation credential rather than to paperwork reconciliation.

The proposal is to prove it in a ninety-day pilot with three or four willing DISCOMs.

Section five. The way forward.

From the end-August submission, three waves. The pilots keep running throughout.

Now: the strategy and architecture documents are submitted, schema contributions are open through Contribute to I E S, and the Minister and Secretary are being briefed.

On governance set-up: the Governance Committee and the I E S Cell stand up, C E A vetting begins through the Chief Architect's Group, schema ratification and versioning move onto the I E S Git, and collaboration with N P C I and the Powerthon technology providers begins — with a body that can actually contract.

And from September, with the R F P: the R F P is issued and a system integrator comes on board, the forward deployment team takes utilities live, regional workshops run with the next cohort, and that cohort onboards in the thirty-day format. S L D Cs, transmission companies and generation companies enter here.

Every item raised in the task force sits in one of these three waves, with an owner. Three start now, and nothing else waits on anything but the submission itself.

One proposed initiative, and it can start next week.

Pledge one: "We will be I E S Ready." An organisation commits to implement the specifications and certify for at least one use case. That is open to utilities, A M I S Ps, technology providers, banks and lenders.

Pledge two: "We will contribute." An organisation commits to develop and review schemas in the open, on the I E S GitHub. That is how the standard grows beyond the founding team.

Government bodies are invited to pledge first — N E G D, M N R E and others alongside utilities and providers, so that public institutions lead by example. I D A has offered to help carry the pledge to more DISCOMs.

A pledge converts interest into named commitment. When governance stands up, it inherits a public register of organisations already committed to implement, and to contribute.

Built once. Used by everyone.

Working alone, every stakeholder solves the same problem by itself. With one common standard, the work is done once, and every participant can use it.

It is live now, with four pilots building. It is open — published specifications, no licence fee. And it is yours to keep: your systems stay exactly as they are.
